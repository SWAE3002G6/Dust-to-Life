Awareness project for Oman

Protect Oman’s Native Trees — Help Stop Desertification

Native trees such as Sidr and Samar are not just part of Oman’s landscape. They protect soil, support wildlife, reduce erosion, and help communities keep land healthier for the future.

How to Report References

Why this campaign matters

Desertification means land in dry areas loses its productivity and ecological value. It is connected to soil erosion, vegetation loss, drought pressure, unsustainable land use, and climate stress. The UN Convention to Combat Desertification describes desertification as a major challenge for sustainable development, especially in drylands where communities depend on healthy soil and vegetation.[1]

In Oman, protecting wild plants is directly connected to national environmental work. The Environment Authority’s national initiative to plant 10 million trees was launched to raise awareness, preserve wild plants, combat desertification, and increase green cover in the Sultanate.[2] This means that protecting existing native trees is just as important as planting new ones.

Native trees are natural protection systems

Native trees such as Sidr and Samar are adapted to dry environments. Their roots help hold soil in place, their canopies reduce wind speed near the ground, and their shade lowers surface heat. When trees are removed, soil is more exposed to wind and water erosion, which can accelerate land degradation.[3]

Tree cover also supports biodiversity. Birds, insects, small mammals, reptiles, and grazing animals can depend on native trees for shelter, food, nesting spaces, and shade. Losing one mature tree can reduce habitat quality in a whole local area, especially in dry landscapes where vegetation is naturally limited.

How cutting trees increases desertification

When native trees are cut without permission, the land loses roots that bind soil, shade that protects seedlings, and organic matter from leaves and branches. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations explains that desertification and land degradation are serious challenges because they increase poverty, food insecurity, forced migration risk, and vulnerability to extreme weather.[4]

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change links land degradation processes, including deforestation and vegetation loss, with reduced carbon sinks and higher environmental pressure.[5] In a dry country like Oman, even small losses of natural vegetation can matter because recovery may be slow.

Omani law and why reporting matters

Omani Ministerial Decision 128/93 prohibits cutting green trees and also prohibits collecting or transporting dry trees/firewood unless a permit is issued by the competent authority.[6] The same decision states that cutting green trees or collecting or transporting firewood without a permit is punishable by a fine of 200 Omani Rials, with fines doubled for repeated violations.[6]

This legal protection shows that tree cutting is not only an environmental issue; it is also a public responsibility. Reporting helps authorities respond, document violations, and prevent repeated damage in the same area.

Important: Do not confront anyone directly. Your safety comes first. Report through official channels and provide clear information only when it is safe to do so.

How to report illegal tree cutting in Oman

  1. Stay safe. Do not argue with the person and do not put yourself in danger.
  2. Record the location. Save the GPS location, nearby village/wadi/road name, or a clear landmark.
  3. Write the time and date. This helps the authority verify the incident.
  4. Collect evidence safely. A photo or short video can help, but only if it is safe and legal to take it.
  5. Report to official channels. The Environment Authority website includes “Notify & Report” and “Report Wildlife” services, and its website lists the official contact number 24404696.[7] Published reports quoting the Environment Authority also mention hotline 1991 for environmental violations and wildlife infringements.[8]

What every person can do

Protect, do not remove

A mature native tree can take many years to grow. Before removing any tree, people should check whether a permit is required and whether the tree can be protected instead. Keeping existing native trees is often more effective than planting new ones because older trees already support soil and wildlife.

Plant native species

When planting, choose native or locally suitable species and avoid planting in ways that waste water. Oman’s national tree-planting initiative focuses on wild trees to combat desertification, preserve biodiversity, and increase green cover.[2]

Teach others

Awareness can prevent violations before they happen. Families, schools, and local communities can explain that cutting trees damages soil, reduces shade, harms animals, and can lead to legal penalties.

Report responsibly

Responsible reporting means giving facts, not rumors. Share the location, time, type of activity, and evidence if available. Do not publish people’s private information online; send reports to official authorities.

Short message for visitors

Every native tree matters. A tree may look small in the landscape, but it can protect soil, shelter wildlife, reduce heat, and slow land degradation. By refusing illegal cutting, planting wisely, and reporting violations safely, we can help protect Oman’s natural heritage for future generations.
